## 1. Regime detection for equities

### 1.1 VIX level and VIX/VIX3M term structure
- The VIX/VIX3M ratio is the standard front-month vs 3-month term-structure regime signal. Ratio < 1.0 = contango (calm/normal — front-month vol priced below medium-term, typical "risk-on" backdrop); ratio > 1.0 = backwardation (near-term fear priced above medium-term — has historically accompanied major dislocations since 2008). Sources: thetrading.tools VIX term-structure tracker (https://www.thetrading.tools/vix-term-structure), SystemTrader VIX tool (https://www.systemtrader.co/tools/vix), Cboe VIX Term Structure page (https://www.cboe.com/tradable-products/vix/term-structure/).
- Backwardation spikes that coincide with sharp SPY drawdowns often mark capitulation lows rather than the start of extended declines (2015, 2018, 2024 examples cited) — i.e., term-structure inversion is a *contrarian, mean-reversion* signal when combined with breadth thrusts and credit-spread tightening, not simply "risk off, de-risk." Source: SystemTrader / thetrading.tools trackers (above).
- Medium/technical treatment of building regime classifiers directly off the VIX term structure curve: Cristian Velasquez, "Detecting VIX Term Structure Regimes" (https://medium.com/@crisvelasquez/detecting-vix-term-structure-regimes-8f3b1a4ddf15).
- Academic modeling of VIX term-structure regimes and their macro drivers via a "Financial Chaos Index": arXiv 2504.18958 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.18958).
- Macrosynergy applies VIX term structure as a systematic trading signal (cross-asset framing): https://macrosynergy.com/research/vix-term-structure-as-a-trading-signal/

**Implementation note (data source constraint for this bot):** VIX and VIX3M are only available via yfinance in this stack per the project's T4 data-tier rule (not on Alpaca/FMP) — this is a known constraint, not new information; flag it in the design rather than silently defaulting to T4 without the mandated fallback alert.

### 1.2 Realized-volatility regimes
- Standard approach: rolling realized vol (e.g., 10/20-day close-to-close or Parkinson/Garman-Klass range-based estimators) bucketed into terciles/quartiles, or fed into an HMM (below) rather than fixed cutoffs. Realized semi-variance (upside vs downside realized vol) has been shown to improve VIX term-structure forecasting: Wang et al., "VIX term structure forecasting: new evidence based on the realized semi-variances," ScienceDirect (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521922001600) — separating "good" (upside) vs "bad" (downside) realized variance materially improves term-structure regime forecasts versus using total realized vol alone.

### 1.3 Hidden Markov Models (HMM)
- HMMs are the standard unsupervised regime-detection tool in the retail/prop-quant literature: they model the market as switching between latent states (e.g., calm/turbulent, or bull/bear/high-vol) each with its own return/vol distribution, and output a *filtered probability* of being in each state today rather than a hard label. QuantStart has two canonical walkthroughs: "Market Regime Detection using Hidden Markov Models in QSTrader" (https://www.quantstart.com/articles/market-regime-detection-using-hidden-markov-models-in-qstrader/) and "Hidden Markov Models for Regime Detection using R" (https://www.quantstart.com/articles/hidden-markov-models-for-regime-detection-using-r/).
- Practical guidance repeatedly found: K=2 or K=3 states is the empirical sweet spot for equities — K=2 captures "calm vs turbulent" (most of the vol-of-vol), K=3 adds a "transitional" state that acts as an early warning ahead of a full stress regime. Source: QuantifiedStrategies overview (https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/hidden-markov-model-market-regimes-how-hmm-detects-market-regimes-in-trading-strategies/), ARIA Analyst blog (https://ariaanalyst.pro/blog/hidden-markov-models-trading).
- A key practical pattern from the literature: train/tune features, hyperparameters, and entry/exit thresholds *separately per regime*, and blend/switch using the HMM's *filtered state probabilities* (soft, continuous) rather than a single hard regime label — this avoids discontinuous whipsaws at regime boundaries. Same QuestDB/ARIA sources above.
- Academic regime-switching factor work: "Regime-Switching Factor Investing with Hidden Markov Models," MDPI (https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/13/12/311) — shows a regime-switching strategy outperforming static strategies out-of-sample in a global all-equity portfolio.
- Practical risk-management use case directly relevant to Confluence 2.0: use the HMM state as a *veto/floor* gate (disallow/derate new entries when regime probability of the high-vol state crosses a threshold) rather than as a scoring input per se — mirrors this bot's existing MRI/size-floor architecture (Architecture Invariant #9), so an HMM state can slot in as an *additional* background regime feed without replacing MRI.

### 1.4 Trend / breadth regimes
- TraderMonty breadth CSV (already an approved T3 source in this project) and sector uptrend ratios are the standard "internal market health" regime proxies, complementing VIX/vol regime detection with participation breadth (how many stocks are actually confirming the move). This project already has `data/breadth.py`/`market-breadth-analyzer` skill infrastructure for this.
- Dispersion vs. correlation is a related, less commonly implemented regime axis: high cross-sectional dispersion + low pairwise correlation = "stock-picker's market" (idiosyncratic regime, favors bottom-up scoring); low dispersion + high correlation = "macro-driven" regime (index-level factors dominate, idiosyncratic scoring has less signal). Source: SpotGamma, "Dispersion, Correlation, Volatility and the Stock Bubble" (https://spotgamma.com/dispersion-correlation-volatility-and-the-stock-bubble/).

### 1.5 Credit spreads (HYG/LQD)
- HYG/LQD ratio (or the z-score of that ratio) is the standard free-data proxy for high-yield credit-spread stress and is already an MRI input in this bot (Architecture Invariant references cross-asset MRI components). Live tracker: https://www.thetrading.tools/credit-spreads / https://www.systemtrader.co/tools/credit-spreads.
- Important caveat directly relevant to over-trusting this signal: credit-spread widening is a useful but *inconsistent* leading indicator — it worked well in credit-driven episodes (2007-08 GFC, 2015-16 energy-sector credit stress) but performed poorly as an early-warning in discount-rate-driven equity drawdowns (2022) or pure vol/leverage events (2020 COVID crash, 2024 vol spike). Source: thetrading.tools (above).
- Recommended practice from the same source: never gate on HYG/LQD alone — combine with VIX and breadth; convergence across all three (credit widening + VIX rising + breadth deteriorating) is "far more reliable than any single read." This directly supports the project's existing 8-component MRI design rather than adding a single new hard gate.
- Historical spread-level anchors (informational, not to be hardcoded as static thresholds per this project's No-Static-Regimes rule): OAS >800bp has coincided with/preceded recession with high historical reliability since the 1990s; OAS <350bp signals late-cycle complacency. Source: thetrading.tools credit tracker (above).

### 1.6 Signal performance conditional on regime — the two anchor papers
- **Daniel & Moskowitz, "Momentum Crashes"** (NBER WP 20439, published JFE 2016): momentum strategies suffer infrequent but severe crashes that are *not random* — they occur specifically in "panic states": following market declines, when realized/implied vol is elevated, and contemporaneous with sharp market rebounds (losers have option-like, high-beta payoffs that spike violently when the market rebounds from a decline). The crashes are partly forecastable ex ante. Their fix: a *dynamic* momentum strategy that scales exposure using forecasts of momentum's own conditional mean and variance (not just market vol) roughly *doubles* the alpha and Sharpe ratio of the static version. Directly relevant: any momentum/trend component in Confluence 2.0 should be down-weighted (not just position-sized down) specifically in "post-decline, high-vol" regime states, not merely "high VIX" in isolation. Sources: NBER (https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439), full PDF (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf), SSRN (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2371227).
- **Moreira & Muir, "Volatility-Managed Portfolios"** (NBER WP 22208): scaling exposure to a factor by 1/σ²ₜ (realized variance over the recent window) — i.e., cutting risk when trailing vol is high and raising it when trailing vol is low — produced large positive alphas and materially higher Sharpe ratios across market, value, momentum, profitability, and other equity factors plus FX carry, historically. Counterintuitive mechanism: vol tends to spike *after* the onset of a recession/crisis, exactly when naive intuition says "don't sell," yet the vol-scaled strategy still won historically because the expected-return-per-unit-of-risk was worse in high-vol states than the extra realized vol implied. Sources: NBER (https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208), full PDF (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22208/w22208.pdf).
- **Important caveat / replication concern:** More recent work casts doubt on the robustness of the original Moreira-Muir result — a broader out-of-sample study found volatility-scaling improved only 5 of 9 original equity factors, and in an expanded 103-portfolio sample, vol-managed versions outperformed in only 53/103 cases vs. 50/103 for the unmanaged versions — essentially a coin flip out of the original sample. This is a **verified, citable de-rating of a famous result**, not a hypothesis — treat 1/σ² exposure scaling as a *candidate* input to test rigorously in this project's own walk-forward framework (Section 4), not as an established free lunch. Source (ScienceDirect follow-up study, "On the performance of volatility-managed portfolios," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X2030132X).

## 2. Dynamic weighting approaches

### 2.1 Exponentially weighted / rolling Information Coefficient (IC) weighting
- Practitioner-standard, low-complexity approach directly suited to small-sample daily retraining: compute the cross-sectional or time-series IC (correlation between each feature's/signal's prediction and realized forward return) over a rolling window, and use those rolling ICs (optionally exponentially decayed, more weight on recent) as the blending weights across signals — no separate forecasting model is needed. Source: arXiv 2508.18592, "Combined machine learning for stock selection strategy based on dynamic weighting methods" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.18592); LSEG/Lipper Alpha, "Enhancing Quant Strategies With Dynamic Factor Weighting" (https://lipperalpha.refinitiv.com/2011/10/enhancing-quant-strategies-with-dynamic-factor-weighting/).
- More rigorous online-learning framing: Recursive Least Squares (RLS) solves the exact exponentially-weighted least-squares regression update at each new observation, trading faster adaptation for more numerical fragility (needs regularization/forgetting-factor tuning) — a natural fit for a few-hundred-sample daily-retrain regime because it updates incrementally rather than re-fitting from scratch. Source: arXiv 2404.08136, "Exponentially Weighted Moving Models" (https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08136v1).
- General online-learning survey context (multiplicative-weights / exponential-weights family, the theoretical basis for exponentially-weighted IC blending): "The Many Faces of Exponential Weights in Online Learning" (http://proceedings.mlr.press/v75/hoeven18a/hoeven18a.pdf).

### 2.2 Regime-switching weights / ensembles
- The HMM literature (Section 1.3) converges on a specific pattern directly usable here: maintain separate weight vectors (or even separate simple models) per detected regime, and blend them using the *current filtered regime probability* as a continuous mixing weight — this is functionally a regime-conditioned ensemble/mixture-of-experts, not a hard switch. Sources as in 1.3.
- MDPI regime-switching factor paper (1.3) is direct evidence this outperforms a single static-weight model out-of-sample for a global equity factor portfolio.

### 2.3 Meta-labeling (Lopez de Prado)
- Meta-labeling is the standard architecture for separating "should we take a signal at all" from "how big should the bet be": a primary model/signal picks direction/side, then a secondary ("meta") model predicts the *probability the primary signal is correct*, and that probability is what drives position sizing (higher confidence → larger size), rather than trying to make one model do both jobs. This directly maps onto Confluence 2.0's architecture: keep the existing confluence score as the primary "side" signal, and consider a lightweight meta-model (even simple rolling logistic regression) whose job is only to estimate P(primary signal correct | current regime features) and feed that into the Kelly-fraction sizing step — this is a much smaller, more sample-efficient learning problem than re-deriving the whole score. Sources: Hudson & Thames, "Does Meta Labeling Add to Signal Efficacy?" (https://hudsonthames.org/does-meta-labeling-add-to-signal-efficacy-triple-barrier-method/); Wikipedia "Meta-Labeling" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-Labeling).
- Meta-labeling is paired in the literature with the **triple-barrier labeling method**: each trade is labeled by whichever of profit-target / stop-loss / max-holding-time barrier is touched first, with barrier widths set from volatility (e.g., ATR-scaled) rather than fixed percentages — producing labels that match how the position mechanically resolves rather than an arbitrary fixed-horizon return. This is the natural labeling scheme for the daily-retrain classifier feeding Confluence 2.0's swing entries, since the bot already uses ATR-based stops/targets. Sources: Hudson & Thames, Quantreo newsletter explainer (https://www.newsletter.quantreo.com/p/the-triple-barrier-labeling-of-marco), Wikipedia Meta-Labeling page.
- **Critical validation implication of triple-barrier labels (feeds directly into Section 3):** because each label's outcome depends on price action over a multi-day forward window, adjacent labels' windows *overlap* — this overlap is exactly the leakage source that purged/embargoed CV is designed to close (Section 3.1).

### 2.4 Practitioner Bayesian-updating framing
- No single canonical "Bayesian regime weighting" practitioner paper surfaced distinct from the HMM/meta-labeling literature above; treat rolling-IC exponential weighting (2.1) and HMM-filtered-probability blending (1.3/2.2) as the two dominant, well-evidenced practitioner approaches for small-sample, frequently-retrained systems, and meta-labeling (2.3) as the sizing-layer complement. A from-scratch full Bayesian hierarchical weight-updating scheme was not found as an evidenced practitioner standard in this search pass and should not be presented as one.

## 3. Continual/daily retraining pitfalls and fixes

### 3.1 Purged and embargoed walk-forward cross-validation (the core fix)
- **The leakage problem, precisely stated:** in financial ML, if a training-set observation's label window overlaps in time with a test-set observation's label window, information leaks across the train/test boundary even in a "clean" chronological split, because the labels are not independent (they're derived from overlapping future price paths). Even *after* removing (purging) directly overlapping observations, a training sample immediately adjacent to the test period can still leak via serial correlation (financial time series exhibit volatility clustering/autocorrelation) — hence the need for an **embargo**: a small additional time gap after the purge boundary before the test window starts. Source: Wikipedia "Purged cross-validation" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation"), summarizing López de Prado's *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (2018) methodology (book TOC/excerpt: https://toc.library.ethz.ch/objects/pdf03/e01_978-1-119-48208-6_01.pdf).
- **Walk-forward validation** replaces a single fixed train/test split with multiple rolling- or expanding-window splits (each still purged+embargoed), reporting a *distribution* of out-of-sample metrics rather than one number from one arbitrarily-lucky/unlucky period — directly matches "retrains daily" since each day's retrain is naturally one more walk-forward fold.
- **Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (CPCV)**, also from López de Prado, generalizes this further by generating multiple combinations of train/test splits from the available data (rather than one forward path), producing many out-of-sample paths from the same dataset — recommended specifically for small-sample regimes where a single walk-forward path gives too few independent test observations to trust. Sources: GitHub issue on validation rigor (https://github.com/CRS5226/StockVeda/issues/28), Scribd summary of the CPCV SSRN paper (https://www.scribd.com/document/725401650/SSRN-id4778909), Medium walkthrough with code (https://antonio-velazquez-bustamante.medium.com/kfold-cross-validation-with-purging-and-embargo-the-ultimate-cross-validation-technique-for-time-2d656ea6f476).
- **Sample-uniqueness weighting** (also López de Prado, from the same *AFML* framework, not separately re-verified via a fresh source this pass but consistent with the purging literature above): down-weight training samples whose label windows overlap heavily with many other samples' windows, since those samples are effectively "the same observation" repeated and would otherwise be over-counted by the loss function.

### 3.2 Concept drift and monitoring
- Distinguish **data drift** (input feature distributions shift) from **concept drift** (the same inputs now map to a different correct output/label) — PSI, KL/JS divergence, the KS test, and Wasserstein distance are all distribution-shift detectors and *do not by themselves* detect concept drift; concept drift requires direct, ongoing accuracy/calibration monitoring against realized outcomes (a continuously-updated "golden set" of resolved trades). Source: DataCamp, "Understanding Data Drift and Model Drift" (https://www.datacamp.com/tutorial/understanding-data-drift-model-drift); Fiddler AI, "Measuring Data Drift with PSI" (https://www.fiddler.ai/blog/measuring-data-drift-population-stability-index).
- Practical split of detector types: KS test and chi-square test are best at flagging *whether* a distribution changed at all (a binary yes/no); PSI, Jensen-Shannon divergence, and Wasserstein distance are better at quantifying *how much* it changed (a magnitude), which is what you want for a graduated pruning decision rather than a hard cutoff. Source: Evidently AI comparison, "Which test is best? We compared 5 methods to detect data drift" (https://www.evidentlyai.com/blog/data-drift-detection-large-datasets).
- **Calibration monitoring (Brier score / Expected Calibration Error):** Brier score is the mean squared error between a model's predicted probability and the realized binary outcome (strictly proper scoring rule — lower is better, and it cannot be gamed by hedging toward 0.5). ECE bins predictions by confidence level and measures the average gap between stated confidence and realized accuracy in each bin. Both are the standard pair for monitoring whether a probability-producing model (e.g., a meta-labeling win-probability model) is still well-calibrated in production, as opposed to just directionally accurate. Sources: futureagi.com glossary (https://futureagi.com/glossary/model-calibration/), Medium calibration guide (https://medium.com/@cartelgouabou/enhancing-medical-predictions-a-comprehensive-guide-to-model-calibration-3ea741be88d7).

### 3.3 Overfitting to recent data — the daily-retrain-specific risk
- Daily retraining on a small sample (few hundred labels) is exactly the regime where a model can chase very recent noise if the retrain isn't constrained: this is the direct real-world justification for combining (a) exponential decay rather than a hard rolling cutoff (so old information fades gracefully rather than vanishing abruptly), (b) purged/embargoed CV on every retrain (not just once at initial build) to get an honest out-of-sample read each day, and (c) the multiple-testing corrections in Section 4 so that "found a good weight combination today" isn't mistaken for a real edge.

## 4. Multiple-testing controls (critical for a system that retrains and re-optimizes daily)

### 4.1 Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) / CSCV — Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, Zhu
- PBO is defined as the probability that a strategy configuration selected as "best" in-sample would rank *below the median* out-of-sample. Under their Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV) framework — a model-free, nonparametric method — this probability approaches 1 as the number of tested configurations (N) grows, *regardless of whether any individual configuration has genuine predictive power*. This is the single most important structural warning for Confluence 2.0's design: the more parameter/weight combinations the daily retrain effectively searches over, the more likely the "best" one found each day is pure in-sample noise, independent of whether real edges exist among the tested set. Sources: SSRN (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253), full PDF (https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf), R package README (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pbo/readme/README.html).

### 4.2 Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) — Bailey & López de Prado
- DSR corrects an observed Sharpe ratio for two specific inflation sources: (1) selection bias from multiple trials (if you try N variants and keep the best, the *expected maximum* Sharpe under the null of "no real edge" rises with N — DSR nets this out using the expected max under the null), and (2) non-normality of the return distribution (skew/kurtosis both distort the naive Sharpe's significance). Directly actionable: before treating any weight/parameter configuration surfaced by the daily retrain as validated, compute its DSR against the *actual number of configurations effectively searched* that day/week, not just look at the raw Sharpe. Sources: SSRN (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551), Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflated_Sharpe_ratio), full PDF (https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf).

### 4.3 Harvey, Liu & Zhu — the t-stat hurdle
- Given the scale of data-mining across the published factor-investing literature (hundreds of papers, hundreds of proposed factors), the traditional significance bar of t-stat > 2.0 is far too lenient; the paper derives a multiple-testing-adjusted historical hurdle and argues a newly discovered factor needs **t-stat > 3.0** to be taken seriously, with most claimed factor findings in the literature likely false at that corrected bar. Directly relevant: any newly-added Confluence 2.0 feature/weight should be judged against this stricter threshold, not the conventional 1.96/2.0 rule of thumb, given how many candidate features and regimes this system will implicitly test over time. Sources: NBER (https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592), full PDF (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20592/w20592.pdf), Duke mirror (https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P118_and_the_cross.PDF). Note a partial rebuttal exists in the literature (arXiv 2206.15365, "Most claimed statistical findings in cross-sectional return predictability are likely true") arguing the field's true positive rate is higher than Harvey-Liu-Zhu implies — flagging this as a genuine, unresolved academic disagreement rather than picking a side.

### 4.4 False discovery rate (FDR)
- Not separately deep-dived this pass beyond the above three (PBO/CSCV, DSR, Harvey-Liu-Zhu) which are the dominant, most-cited practitioner tools in this space; FDR control (Benjamini-Hochberg-style) is the standard complement when testing many individual features/hypotheses simultaneously (as opposed to many whole-strategy configurations, which PBO/DSR target) — recommend applying FDR control specifically at the individual-feature-significance level (Section 6, decay/pruning rule) and PBO/DSR at the whole-model/configuration level.

## 5. Transaction cost modeling for small accounts

- Cost components: commissions/fees, bid-ask spread, slippage (execution price worse than expected due to market movement between decision and fill), and market impact (the trade's own effect on price). Source: QuestDB glossary (https://questdb.com/glossary/slippage-and-market-impact-estimation/), Bocconi BSIC primer (https://bsic.it/modelling-transaction-costs-and-market-impact/).
- Standard cost-model spectrum from simplest to most realistic: fixed cost (per trade) + linear variable cost (models spread/slippage reasonably well) → piecewise-linear → quadratic (most accurate for market impact specifically, since impact is empirically non-linear/convex in order size relative to volume). Source: BSIC "Backtesting Series Episode 5: Transaction Cost Modelling" (https://bsic.it/backtesting-series-episode-5-transaction-cost-modelling/).
- **Directly relevant point for this project's account size:** "a correct and precise estimate of trading costs cannot be separated from the amount of capital a strategy is assumed to run on" — small accounts are disproportionately exposed to fixed-cost and spread components relative to larger accounts, because those costs don't scale down with size the way market impact does. At ~$2.5K-$25K and a handful of shares per trade on liquid large caps, market impact is likely negligible, but spread + slippage as a *percentage of the smaller position's expected profit* is proportionally larger than for an institutional book — meaning the **minimum required edge after costs** is a higher bar (in percentage-of-trade terms) for this account than it would be at scale. Source: QuestDB, BSIC (above).
- Order-type matters: market orders fill fast but at whatever price is available (worse expected slippage); limit orders control price but risk non-fills, which is itself an opportunity cost that needs to be modeled, not ignored. Source: QuestDB glossary (above).
- No small-account-specific empirical minimum-edge-in-bps figure was found as a citable, evidenced number in this pass — flagging that as **[not found / would need empirical measurement against this bot's actual realized fills]** rather than guessing a number, per this project's no-guess mandate. Recommend deriving it empirically from this bot's own Alpaca fill data (arrival price vs. fill price) rather than importing an institutional TCA benchmark that doesn't apply at this size.

## 6. Point-in-time universe construction and survivorship bias for free data

- Survivorship bias (backtesting only on currently-listed/surviving names) is well-documented to inflate backtested returns; one source estimates that 10 years back, a "current constituents only" dataset is missing roughly 75% of the names that were actually trading at that historical date in North America. Source: LuxAlgo, "Survivorship Bias in Backtesting Explained" (https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/survivorship-bias-in-backtesting-explained/).
- Quoted magnitude of the effect on returns/Sharpe varies by study cited: one estimate puts overstated annual returns at 1-4%; another (same search pass, different source) estimates a smaller 0.1-0.9% annual distortion — **these two figures disagree by roughly an order of magnitude and should be treated as a wide, uncertain range, not a single number** [hypothesis-range, not independently re-derived]. Sources: dev.to overview (https://dev.to/pickuma/why-your-backtest-is-lying-to-you-data-biases-that-ruin-equity-research-22hd), Optuma KB (https://www.optuma.com/kb/optuma/data/end-of-day-data-options/survivorship-bias-free-data).
- Standard fix: define the trading universe as point-in-time ("the Russell 3000/S&P 500 as of date X," not "as of today") and explicitly retain names that were later delisted/merged/failed within that historical universe, accepting that their data will be noisier/incomplete near their delisting event. Source: QuantRocket "A Primer on Survivorship Bias" (https://www.quantrocket.com/blog/survivorship-bias/), sharpely.in bias-free backtesting explainer (https://sharpely.in/blogs/bias-free-backtesting-explained-sharpely-uses-point-time-data-avoid-look/).
- **Direct implication for this project's free/low-cost data stack (Alpaca T1, FMP T2, TraderMonty T3):** none of these were verified in this research pass to explicitly guarantee delisted-name inclusion for point-in-time universe construction — this should be checked directly against Alpaca's and FMP's asset/data documentation (not assumed) before trusting any historical backtest universe built from them as survivorship-bias-free. Flagging as an open verification item rather than asserting either way.

## 7. Concrete, implementable recommendations for Confluence 2.0

### 7.1 Regime features to compute (from data already in or planned for this stack)
1. **VIX/VIX3M ratio** (contango/backwardation) + its rate of change — T4/yfinance source per existing data-tier rules; log the fallback per Rule 5.5 as this project already mandates.
2. **Realized volatility regime**: rolling 10-day and 20-day realized vol on SPY from Alpaca daily bars (T1, already compliant), bucketed via terciles computed on a trailing 1-2yr window (not fixed absolute cutoffs — consistent with this project's No-Static-Regimes rule), optionally split into upside/downside realized semi-variance per the ScienceDirect finding (1.2).
3. **2-3 state HMM** fit on SPY daily returns + realized vol (and optionally VIX level) — output the *filtered state probability* (not a hard label) as a continuous background regime feature, refit on the same daily/weekend cadence as the rest of Confluence 2.0.
4. **Breadth regime**: TraderMonty sector-uptrend ratio (T3, already available) as a participation/internals check alongside price-based signals.
5. **Credit-spread regime**: HYG/LQD ratio z-score (already an MRI input) — per the reviewed caveat (1.5), never gate alone; require confirmation from VIX + breadth before treating credit stress as decisive.
6. **Dispersion/correlation regime** (net-new): rolling pairwise correlation or cross-sectional return dispersion across this bot's tradable universe, from Alpaca daily bars (T1) — flags whether the market favors idiosyncratic (confluence-style) picking vs. macro-dominated moves; not currently in this project's MRI per the codebase review context available in this task.

### 7.2 Dynamic-weighting method best suited to small samples + daily retrain
- **Primary recommendation:** exponentially-weighted rolling Information Coefficient (IC) blending (Section 2.1) as the core weight-update mechanism — it is the simplest, most sample-efficient, and most literature-supported method for a few-hundred-label regime with daily updates, requires no separate model to be fit, and degrades gracefully (weights shrink toward zero for a decaying-IC feature rather than the model breaking).
- **Layer regime-conditioning on top** (Section 2.2/2.3): use the HMM's filtered regime probability as a *continuous multiplier* on the exponentially-weighted IC blend (e.g., maintain separate rolling-IC weight sets per regime bucket, mixed by current filtered probability) rather than either (a) one global weight set ignoring regime, or (b) a hard regime switch.
- **Add meta-labeling as the sizing layer, not the direction layer** (Section 2.3): keep the existing confluence score/direction call as the primary signal; introduce a small, separately-trained meta-model (simple logistic regression is enough at this sample size) whose sole job is P(primary signal resolves as a win | current regime features), retrained on the same cadence, output feeding the existing Kelly-fraction position-sizing step. This isolates the higher-risk "should we trust this regime enough to size up" learning problem from the core scoring logic, and is a much smaller, more identifiable learning target than re-deriving the whole score from scratch daily.

### 7.3 Validation protocol
- **CV scheme:** purged + embargoed walk-forward (Section 3.1) as the minimum bar; move to Combinatorial Purged CV (CPCV) once there is enough historical label volume that a single walk-forward path is judged too noisy (a judgment call to make once real trade-label counts are known — do not hardcode a sample-count trigger without checking against this project's actual accumulated label count).
- **Embargo length:** must be set relative to the label horizon actually used by the triple-barrier method (i.e., the max holding period barrier) — the purge removes any training label whose window overlaps the test window; the embargo then adds extra buffer beyond that to cover residual serial correlation from volatility clustering. Recommend deriving embargo length empirically (autocorrelation decay of realized vol on this bot's traded universe) rather than importing a rule-of-thumb number, consistent with the no-guess mandate — this specific number was **not found** as a citable universal constant in this research pass.
- **Acceptance thresholds:** require (a) PBO below an explicit, pre-committed ceiling (the CSCV framework produces this number directly, Section 4.1), (b) Deflated Sharpe Ratio, not raw Sharpe, positive and significant against the *actual* number of configurations searched (Section 4.2), and (c) — for any brand-new feature being considered for the score — a t-stat hurdle of 3.0, not 2.0, on its IC (Section 4.3), acknowledging the cited academic pushback on that exact threshold (2206.15365).
- **Calibration monitoring:** track Brier score and ECE for the meta-labeling win-probability output on a rolling basis (Section 3.2) — a degrading Brier/ECE trend is an earlier, more direct signal that the meta-model's confidence is drifting than any input-distribution drift test would catch.

### 7.4 Monitoring/decay rule for feature pruning
- Combine three signals, not one: (i) rolling exponentially-weighted IC trending toward/through zero (direct decay of the feature's own predictive power — ties directly into the weighting mechanism itself, so a decaying feature naturally self-mutes before any hard prune decision is even needed); (ii) an input-distribution shift test (PSI or KS, Section 3.2) on the feature's own values, flagging when its *regime* has changed even before IC visibly decays; (iii) calibration drift (Brier/ECE, Section 3.2) on the downstream meta-model, as a lagging confirmation that something is wrong even if it's not immediately attributable to one feature.
- Because the exponential-IC-weighting scheme already self-decays a fading feature's *weight* continuously, treat "prune" (fully remove a feature) as a distinct, higher-bar decision from "down-weight" (already happening automatically) — recommend a hard prune only when the rolling IC has been at/below a noise floor (itself informed by the DSR/PBO framework in Section 4, not an arbitrary fixed number) for a sustained multi-week window, consistent with this project's own pattern of requiring sustained (not single-scan) evidence before structural changes (see the existing "sustain-window" design principle already used elsewhere in this codebase's risk-dial work).
